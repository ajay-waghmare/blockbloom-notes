
##############################################################################
TOPIC: Piece-generation fairness and difficulty smoothing in 8x8 block-puzzle games (Block Blast!, Woodoku, Blockudoku, 1010!, Tetris-likes)
##############################################################################
findings: 8 (high/med 8, low 0)

[1] (high) A shipped 8x8 'place-three-pieces-clear-lines' game (Block Peak) guarantees every dealt 3-piece hand has at least one fully solvable placement order, verified by exhaustive recursive search, not by hope.
    DETAIL: Core check `traySolvable(board, pieces)`: if pieces is empty return true; else for each distinct piece p in the remaining set, for each legal (row,col) position of p, place it, clear any full lines, and recurse on the resulting board with p removed from the set — return true if any branch succeeds. Identical-shape branches are pruned to one, keeping the search 'well under a millisecond' on an 8x8 board with 3 pieces. Generation pipeline is 4 stages: (1) generate ~16 candidate hands with piece sizes weighted along a sine-wave difficulty curve; (2) rate each candidate on solvability, max clearable lines (budgeted DFS), legal-placement count, 'edge-contact' fit score, and a shape-repetition penalty; (3) bucket candidates into easy/medium/hard/'rescue' and, on crowded boards, heavily favor 'rescue' hands (solvable AND able to clear lines); (4) below a fill threshold (≤28/64 filled cells) the generator inverts the problem — a best-first DFS searches for a piece sequence that fully empties the board and deals exactly that sequence, replacing randomness with construction for a full-clear moment. If the smart generator finds no solvable candidate in time, it falls back to a simpler generator that constructs a solvable tray piece-by-piece against a live simulated board — 'there is no code path that hands you an unplayable set.' Runtime is wall-clock-boxed (a few ms on flagship phones, 50-150ms on entry-level) so it self-calibrates rather than skip the check under time pressure. Author's stated philosophy: 'The guarantee is that a solution exists, not that you'll find it' — fairness is a floor, not a difficulty setting.
    SRC: https://dev.to/bbeycanov/every-hand-is-solvable-engineering-a-block-puzzle-that-never-cheats-38jp

[2] (medium) Academic work on 1010! formalizes the same problem — random block generation can deal a block-holder set that fits nowhere on the board by any combination, ending the game unfairly — and proposes a two-stage 'performance improver': an AND-OR tree validator plus an 'unfit block changer' that swaps out a block that breaks solvability.
    DETAIL: Per the paper's own abstract: 1010! generates blocks randomly, so block-holders can occasionally hold a combination that cannot fit the board at all, causing an unfair loss. Their fix validates every generated set with an AND-OR tree built over the block set's permutations and each block's candidate board positions (OR nodes = alternative placements/orderings to try, AND nodes = requirement that a full set be simultaneously placeable) before the set is dealt; if validation fails, the 'unfit block changer' stage replaces the offending block. I could not retrieve the full paper (PDF was image-based/unreadable and ResearchGate/Academia.edu blocked direct fetch), so I only have the abstract-level mechanism, not the precise pseudocode or reported catch-rate/performance numbers — treat the algorithmic outline as reliable but incomplete.
    SRC: https://www.ijcte.org/index.php?m=content&c=index&a=show&catid=107&id=1528

[3] (high) Modern Tetris Guideline games (since 2001) use the '7-bag' random generator: put one of each of the 7 tetrominoes in a bag, shuffle, deal them one at a time, refill and reshuffle when empty. This bounds the worst-case wait for any specific piece to 12 pieces (a piece can appear last in one bag and first-again would be the 13th slot, so max drought = 12).
    DETAIL: Pseudocode: bag = shuffle([I,J,L,O,S,T,Z]); loop { if bag empty: bag = shuffle([I,J,L,O,S,T,Z]); emit bag.pop() }. This directly targets 'anti-streak' fairness: no piece can be totally absent for long, and no piece can flood indefinitely (a run of S/Z pieces is capped near 4 in a row). It replaced earlier per-title randomizers that had no such guarantee.
    SRC: https://dinogame.gg/blog/how-tetris-randomizers-work/

[4] (high) Before the 7-bag became standard, Tetris the Grand Master (TGM, Arika, 1998+) used a weaker history-based reroll rule: draw a random piece; if it matches any of the last 4 pieces dealt, reroll (up to 4-6 attempts); if still colliding after the reroll budget, accept the duplicate anyway rather than loop forever.
    DETAIL: This reduces (but does not eliminate) short-run repeats and droughts compared to pure memoryless RNG, but gives a weaker fairness guarantee than 7-bag — droughts are less frequent than pure-random but still occur more often than under bag randomization, because the algorithm only rerolls, it never enforces a hard per-N-pieces quota the way a bag does.
    SRC: https://dinogame.gg/blog/how-tetris-randomizers-work/

[5] (high) Original NES/Game Boy Tetris randomizers were measurably unfair by modern standards: NES uses a memoryless picker with a single reroll-on-immediate-repeat (first-order Markov-ish), and Game Boy combines the last two pieces via bitwise-OR into the pick (second-order), producing real statistical bias and long droughts, not just anecdote.
    DETAIL: 10-million-game simulation results: NES average drought (pieces before a repeat) = 6.2, with a 3.3% chance any given drought exceeds 20 pieces and 0.1% chance it exceeds 40; marginal piece frequencies stay close to the ideal 14.3% (I=13.9%, S=14.7%, T=14.6%). Game Boy's second-order scheme is structurally biased: O/S/T pieces are over-dealt (~16.0-16.1% vs 14.3% ideal) while L is under-dealt (10.7%), and some piece-to-piece transition probabilities drop below 1%, creating 'citation rings' where certain pieces chain into each other. Both are worse than a true geometric drought distribution (baseline 3.9% chance of a 20+ drought).
    SRC: https://babeheim.com/blog/2020-12-29-is-tetris-biased/

[6] (medium) Dynamic difficulty adjustment tied to board fill % (favor small pieces as the board fills, favor large/challenging pieces when the board is empty) is a real, implemented technique in at least the Block Peak clone, applied as a sine-wave pacing curve on top of the hard solvability floor.
    DETAIL: Piece-size weighting shifts with a sine-wave curve across a play session/board state so the game 'breathes: easy in, ramps up, eases off,' and on crowded boards the candidate-rating stage explicitly upweights 'rescue' hands (solvable AND line-clearing). Widely repeated secondary/SEO claims that Block Blast! itself (the specific commercial app) shifts piece-size odds by fill percentage, or deliberately worsens piece odds after a win streak as an 'engagement loop', are NOT independently verified — no official Block Blast developer source was found; these claims come only from content-farm SEO sites (blockblastsolverss.com, playgama.com) synthesizing player suspicion, not disclosed mechanics or reverse-engineering data.
    SRC: https://dev.to/bbeycanov/every-hand-is-solvable-engineering-a-block-puzzle-that-never-cheats-38jp

[7] (medium) When fairness/solvability guarantees are absent or merely suspected to be absent, the single most common player complaint pattern across this genre is: 'the pieces are secretly adversarial, not random' — often specifically triggered by streaks of good play or approaching a personal high score, plus 'the same unusable set repeats.'
    DETAIL: Directly quoted Woodoku user reviews: "the next 3 moves are highly adversarial – meaning, not only are the next set of pieces not random, but as the score goes up, there is a correlation between a higher probability of getting three pieces that yield worst possible outcome"; "the game will give you the exact same blocks in the exact same order, attempt after attempt"; "the game is rigged to get you to lose as quickly as possible"; "there is an artificial ceiling regardless of how well the game is played." Equivalent Block Blast! complaint themes (from secondary/aggregator sources, not independently confirmed): long combo streaks are suddenly followed by an awkward, unplaceable trio right as the board gets tight, which players read as deliberate post-success punishment. These are genuine anecdotal player sentiment (real reviews), but none of the sources prove actual rigging — they show the perception risk of NOT having (or not being seen to have) a hard solvability guarantee, independent of whether real manipulation exists.
    SRC: https://www.complaintsboard.com/woodoku-wood-block-puzzles-b149599

[8] (medium) A separate, publicly available Blockudoku/Woodoku/BlockSudoku engine+solver ('blokie') demonstrates the evaluation heuristics a strong player (or an anti-unfairness evaluator) should optimize for when judging whether a board state is 'in trouble', useful as a difficulty/health metric even though it is not evidence of what the real games' generators do internally.
    DETAIL: The solver treats each dealt set of pieces as one decision, enumerates every legal ordering/placement, and picks the resulting board state that scores best on: (1) minimize total occupied cells, (2) minimize perimeter/jaggedness of occupied regions, (3) avoid isolated single-cell gaps, (4) preserve open 3x3 sub-regions and multiple live paths to clearing a row/column/box. This is an independent third-party solver, not a disclosed or reverse-engineered description of Woodoku/Blockudoku's actual generation algorithm — cite only as a board-health heuristic, not as proof of internal game mechanics.
    SRC: https://github.com/gary-z/blokie

== IMPLICATIONS FOR THIS GAME ==
  → DO build a hard solvability guarantee before anything else in this list — it is the single highest-leverage, lowest-cost fix available and it is exactly what the genre's own worst app-store reviews (Woodoku: 'rigged', 'same blocks every attempt', 'artificial ceiling') are complaining about when it's missing or merely suspected to be missing. On an 8x8 board with a 3-piece hand this check is computationally trivial (the Block Peak author measured well under 1ms) — there is no scale excuse to skip it, at 70 MAU or 70 million.
  → Concrete build: implement the recursive traySolvable(board, pieces) check shown above (try every distinct piece, every legal position, clear lines, recurse on what's left) directly against your real 8x8 board representation (a bitboard/long is a natural fit in Kotlin). If your random hand fails the check, retry generation a bounded number of times; if that still fails, fall back to a constructive generator that places pieces one at a time against the live board so the final hand is solvable by construction, not by luck. That combination is the actual industry-adjacent pattern (matches both the shipped-indie account and the 1010! academic paper's validator+replacer approach) and gives you a mathematically zero unfair-game-over rate.
  → Do NOT build player-skill-segmented DDA (adjusting difficulty based on an individual player's historical performance/analytics). That requires a telemetry pipeline and player model you don't have (you have no analytics yet), it's the mechanism most associated in this research with players feeling cheated when it's detected (the 'punished right after a streak' complaint pattern in both Woodoku and Block Blast reviews looks exactly like what a naive skill-adaptive system produces), and at 70 MAU you have nowhere near the data volume to tune or validate it safely. This is a case where the 'simplest direct answer IS the best outcome' — skip it entirely for now.
  → DO add a lightweight, deterministic difficulty-smoothing layer that needs no player data: weight piece size by current board fill % (favor small/rescue-shaped pieces as the board fills, allow bigger/harder pieces on an empty board) using something like a smooth easy-ramp-ease curve. This is cheap, local, testable, and matches the one concretely-documented technique in this research (Block Peak's sine-wave pacing + rescue-hand bucket) — treat it as smoothing the RNG's rough edges, not as adaptive difficulty, and keep the hard solvability floor as a separate, non-negotiable check underneath it.
  → Add a simple anti-repeat penalty (don't deal the identical shape/size two or three hands in a row) rather than trying to port Tetris's literal 7-bag — your piece set is a much larger polyomino library than Tetris's fixed 7 tetrominoes, so a literal bag doesn't map cleanly; a repetition penalty in your candidate-scoring step gets you the same anti-streak fairness Tetris players get from 7-bag, and directly answers the 'same blocks every time' complaint seen in real Woodoku reviews.
  → Skip building any GDC-style 'analyzing difficulty drivers' data-science pipeline (the kind King/Candy Crush-style teams run) — that infrastructure exists to tune hundreds of hand-authored levels for a huge live-ops team and doesn't apply to an endless single-board 8x8 puzzle with no levels; it would be pure over-engineering relative to the size of this problem.
  → One thing I could not verify and flag honestly: I found no official developer disclosure (blog, patent, interview) from the makers of Block Blast!, Woodoku, or Blockudoku describing their actual internal generation algorithm — everything attributed to those specific commercial titles in this research comes from secondary SEO/aggregator sites summarizing player suspicion, not confirmed mechanics. Treat any claim of the form 'Block Blast's algorithm does X' as unverified; the only concretely-sourced, pseudocode-level algorithms in this research are from an independent shipped indie clone (Block Peak) and an academic paper on 1010!, plus the well-documented Tetris 7-bag/TGM history.

TOPIC: Game feel ("juice") for tile/block clears on mobile — techniques, timing/numbers, and low-end Android caveats for an 8x8 block-puzzle game
##############################################################################
findings: 12 (high/med 11, low 1)

[1] (high) The three canonical references for "juice" are: Vlambeer's Jan Willem Nijman "The Art of Screenshake" (INDIGO Classes 2013), Martin Jonasson & Petri Purho's "Juice it or Lose it" (Nordic Game Jam / GDC 2012), and Mark Brown's Game Maker's Toolkit episode "Secrets of Game Feel and Juice" (Feb 2015), which itself is Brown's synthesis/re-presentation of the Jonasson/Purho talk.
    DETAIL: Nijman's talk takes a dull side-scrolling shooter and applies ~30 incremental tweaks (bullet size, camera behavior, sound bass, screenshake-on-fire) live, showing each tweak's isolated effect. Jonasson/Purho's talk does the same live-coding exercise on Breakout, adding particles, screenshake, sound, slow-mo and color flash one at a time, with source released. Definition used: 'A juicy game feels alive and responds to everything you do — tons of cascading action and response for minimal user input.' GMTK's video is the most accessible modern summary aimed at indie/solo devs specifically.
    SRC: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SkgkIXZ_13Y (Vlambeer); https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fy0aCDmgnxg (Juice it or Lose It); https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=216_5nu4aVQ (GMTK)

[2] (high) The standard, reusable screenshake model is Squirrel Eiserloh's "trauma" system (GDC 2016, "Math for Game Programmers: Juicing Your Cameras With Math"): keep a trauma value in [0,1], drive shake magnitude by trauma² or trauma³ (not trauma linearly), decay trauma linearly over time, and drive the actual offsets with Perlin/simplex noise rather than pure random jitter.
    DETAIL: Formula shape: shake = trauma^2 (or ^3); angle = maxAngle * shake * randomFloat(-1,1); same pattern applied to translational X/Y offsets. Using an exponent >1 means small knocks barely register but stacked/heavy hits escalate fast and 'perceptibly' — worked example in the talk shows trauma at 0.30/0.60/0.90 producing roughly 3%/22%/73% of max shake intensity, i.e. escalation feels non-linear and dramatic near the top. Typical single-event trauma increments cited: +0.2 to +0.5 per hit (so 2-5 stacked hits ramps to near-max). Perlin noise is preferred over random because it's smooth (no jarring single-frame teleports), continues sensibly through pause/slow-mo, and its frequency is an independently tunable knob. For a 2D top-down/board game (not a 3D camera), combine small translational + rotational shake; the talk's recommendation for 3D cameras (rotation-only) doesn't apply to a flat 8x8 grid.
    SRC: https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Eiserloh/GDC2016-Eiserloh_djvu.txt (GDC 2016 talk transcript); slides: http://www.mathforgameprogrammers.com/gdc2016/GDC2016_Eiserloh_Squirrel_JuicingYourCameras.pdf

[3] (medium) Squash-and-stretch (animation principle #1) is the core technique for making a placed/dropped block feel like it has weight and impact: flatten the shape on impact, stretch it on the preceding motion, keep perceived volume roughly constant (wider = shorter).
    DETAIL: Comes from classical animation (Disney's 12 principles) via Steve Swink's 2008 book 'Game Feel: A Game Designer's Guide to Virtual Sensation', the foundational text the juice talks build on. GMTK's interactive 'Platformer Toolkit' exposes squash/stretch as tunable sliders to demonstrate the effect live. No single canonical numeric ratio was found in primary sources — the operative guidance is qualitative (deform on impact, return to rest quickly) rather than a fixed percentage; community Unity/DOTween examples commonly use two chained tweens as a practical starting point: quick squash (~0.05s, ease-in) then rebound to normal scale (~0.15s, ease-out-back).
    SRC: general knowledge (Swink, Game Feel, 2008) + https://gmtk.itch.io/platformer-toolkit — exact numeric ratio unverified; the ~0.05s/0.15s pattern is from https://gamedevcheatsheet.com/dotween-reference (community tutorial, not a canonical spec)

[4] (high) Tetris Guideline gives real, shipped numeric timing benchmarks for piece lock and line-clear pacing that a block-clear game can use as a reference envelope: ARE (entry/spawn delay after lock) = 6 frames ≈ 100ms at 60Hz; standard lock delay = 30 frames = 0.5s (reset on move/rotate); NES line-clear delay ≈ 17-20 frames (~0.28-0.33s) depending on lock frame; Puyo Puyo Tetris uses 30 frames base line-clear delay plus +5 frames per extra line (single), +10 (double/triple), +15 (Tetris/Tetris-plus) — i.e. the clear animation itself intentionally gets slightly longer as the clear gets bigger, up to roughly 0.5-0.75s total.
    DETAIL: These are frame-exact, shipped, guideline-conformant numbers (not folklore), useful as a starting envelope: clear/consequence animations in the 250-750ms range, scaling up modestly with clear size, are proven not to feel laggy in a genre this game is adjacent to. For an 8x8 block-blast style game (not gravity-drop Tetris), the equivalent is: a brief clear animation ~250-400ms for a single line, extending toward ~500-600ms for a multi-line/combo clear — but keep new-piece input available immediately (don't gate the next drag on the animation finishing; see the responsiveness note in implications).
    SRC: https://tetris.wiki/Tetris_Guideline ; https://tetris.fandom.com/wiki/ARE ; https://tetris.fandom.com/wiki/Line_clear ; https://harddrop.com/wiki/Puyo_Puyo_Tetris_timings

[5] (high) The 'ghost piece' (shadow/drop-preview) is the standard, guideline-mandated anticipation cue in the genre: a translucent silhouette shows exactly where the current piece will land, reducing misdrops and letting players plan ahead; it's been part of every Tetris Guideline-conformant release and is a documented UX win, though a minority of purists dislike it as removing a skill element.
    DETAIL: Direct analogue for an 8x8 block-puzzle game: render a semi-transparent copy of the dragged piece snapped to the nearest valid grid cells under the finger, updated every frame during drag, shown in an 'invalid' tint (e.g. red-tinted) when the drop position is illegal. This is table-stakes 'anticipation' juice — its absence is conspicuous in the genre.
    SRC: https://tetris.fandom.com/wiki/Ghost_piece ; https://harddrop.com/wiki/Ghost_piece

[6] (high) Combo escalation is very effectively communicated through audio pitch-shifting the clear sound upward by a semitone (or similar small step) per combo step, using multiplier 2^(n/12) ≈ 1.06^n for a shift of n semitones — this reads as 'climbing' without requiring the player to look at a combo counter.
    DETAIL: Pattern found in an open-source Tetris clone: pitch the clear 'ding' up a semitone per combo step via SetSoundPitch(2^(combo/12)). General sound-design guidance (Game Developer magazine, 'The Power of Pitch Shifting'): 'When several actions are chained together such as in a combo, successively increase the pitch of the associated sound effect' — reinforces chain length and increases satisfaction; a semitone is specifically called out as 'a natural sounding pitch step for a rising sequence.' Practical implementation for an 8x8 game: base clear SFX at 1.0x pitch for combo step 1, ×1.06 per additional step, with a cap (e.g. after ~8-10 steps / about one octave) so it doesn't become shrill or exceed a pleasant range — reset on a miss/new game, or clamp-and-hold at the ceiling for very long chains.
    SRC: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/the-power-of-pitch-shifting ; https://github.com/ggrace519/tetris-c/pull/9

[7] (medium) For score/combo-count popups and other 'juicy UI' feedback, the field convention is a short scale-punch (squash-then-overshoot) rather than a flat fade, using ease-out-back or ease-out-elastic for the celebratory bounce and reserving plain ease-out-cubic/quad for less exciting UI moves; a floating '+N' text that rises and fades over roughly 500-600ms is a commonly cited baseline for a single pop.
    DETAIL: Concrete pattern found: squash `DOScale(0.9, 0.05s, EaseInQuad)` immediately followed by rebound `DOScale(1.0, 0.15s, EaseOutBack)` — i.e. ~200ms total punch. Community guidance: use `easeOutCubic`/`easeOutQuad` for most UI panel motion (feels responsive, no overshoot), `easeOutBack` for tactile button/score-pop moments, and `easeOutElastic` reserved for big celebratory reveals (e.g. a large combo milestone) since elastic overshoot reads as 'more exciting' but can feel gimmicky if overused. A '+1' floating text rising and fading over ~600ms was cited as an effective, simple pattern for point-scored feedback.
    SRC: https://gamedevcheatsheet.com/dotween-reference ; https://blog.febucci.com/2018/08/easing-functions/ ; https://github.com/borisasadanin/Bip/issues/22 — the 600ms figure and specific ease recommendations come from secondary tutorial/community sources, not one authoritative spec

[8] (high) Android's official haptics design guidance gives concrete, implementable rules that map directly onto a block-clear game: use `VibrationEffect` predefined effects or `VibrationEffect.Composition` primitives (not legacy `vibrate(long)`/one-shot APIs); map haptic strength to event importance (very subtle for frequent things like drag/scroll, medium for toggles, strong for significant confirmations, increasing amplitude as a drag approaches/snaps to a valid target); and always co-design haptics with the synchronized visual + audio cue rather than bolting them on separately.
    DETAIL: Direct quote: 'We strongly recommend co-design of visual, audio, and haptic effects. Make it harmonious or congruent with visual animations and sound patterns' — out-of-sync haptics 'feel broken or unsettling.' Also explicit: 'When it comes to haptic feedback on mobile devices, less is more. Too much vibration can be annoying and even numbing...and can also be distracting.' Timing specifics: a good keyclick-style tap should be ~10-20ms of input duration plus an additional ~20-50ms of actuator 'ring-out' to budget for; avoid single-shot long-duration vibrations because they read as 'buzzy' rather than crisp. Amplitude/scale guidance: `VibrationEffect.Composition` scale runs 0.0-1.0 (0.0 is still a perceivable minimum, not off); recommended discrete tiers are roughly 0.5/0.7/1.0 for low/medium/high; a scale ratio difference of ~1.4x or more is needed between two effects for players to perceive them as distinctly different strengths — useful for e.g. distinguishing a normal placement tick from a bigger clear/combo thud.
    SRC: https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/haptics/haptics-principles

[9] (high) The concrete `VibrationEffect.Composition` primitives available for building a placement-tick / clear-thud / combo-wobble haptic vocabulary are: PRIMITIVE_CLICK, PRIMITIVE_THUD, PRIMITIVE_SPIN, PRIMITIVE_SLOW_RISE, PRIMITIVE_QUICK_RISE, PRIMITIVE_QUICK_FALL, PRIMITIVE_LOW_TICK, PRIMITIVE_TICK — composed with per-primitive scale (0-1) and inter-primitive delay in milliseconds.
    DETAIL: Delay-perception guidance: gaps under ~5-10ms are not reliably detectable as separate events; ~50ms+ gaps read as clearly separate; ~100ms+ gaps make a sequence feel like distinct, individual taps rather than one integrated gesture — directly usable for tuning e.g. a 3-line-combo haptic as three closely-spaced ticks (30-50ms apart, reads as one cascading burst) versus a deliberate confirmation double-tap (100ms+ apart). Worked example patterns from Android's own docs: a 'bounce' using repeated PRIMITIVE_THUD with geometrically decreasing scale (0.7^bounceCount) for a settling-impact feel; a 'wobble' using two PRIMITIVE_SPIN plus a PRIMITIVE_THUD at +100ms delay; a drag-resistance pattern using PRIMITIVE_LOW_TICK with scale computed proportional to drag offset (continuous haptic feedback scaling with an in-progress gesture, e.g. dragging a piece near a snap point). Important platform caveat: composition primitives require checking `vibrator.areAllPrimitivesSupported(...)` since there is no automatic fallback — on many budget/older Android devices (common in an India-heavy user base) primitives or even amplitude control may be unsupported, so a same-feeling fallback using plain predefined EFFECT_CLICK/EFFECT_TICK (or legacy one-shot as last resort) is required, not optional.
    SRC: https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/haptics/custom-haptic-effects

[10] (medium) Screenshake (and by extension aggressive haptics) should be a togglable accessibility setting, not a fixed always-on effect, because a meaningful share of players experience motion sickness or discomfort from persistent camera/screen shake.
    DETAIL: Cited examples of shipped games adding shake-disable toggles: Overcooked: All You Can Eat! (camera shake toggle + anti-disorientation dot), Disney Dreamlight Valley (toggle for screen shake and flashing lights), Minecraft (toggles for view bobbing, distortion, FOV effects, damage tilt). One secondary source states motion sickness 'affects up to one third of people' in gaming contexts — this specific statistic comes from an accessibility-advocacy blog, not a peer-reviewed or first-party source, so treat the number itself as unverified even though the general phenomenon and the 'ship a toggle' recommendation are corroborated across multiple shipped-game examples.
    SRC: https://eip.gg/news/accessible-games-for-motion-sickness/ (stat unverified) ; https://madelinemiller.dev/blog/motion-sickness-accessibility/ ; https://steamcommunity.com/app/2767030/discussions/0/803467095729975829/

[11] (high) On low-end Android hardware, the dominant risks to perceived performance from 'juice' work are GPU overdraw from particle/flash effects (especially anything with alpha < 1 layered repeatedly) and Compose recomposition churn from driving many animated composables at once — both are directly measurable and both have official mitigations.
    DETAIL: From Android's own Compose performance guidance: overdraw happens when the GPU redraws the same pixel multiple times (background + shadow + another background, etc.); transparent/alpha layers are 'especially expensive' because each overlapping pixel needs extra blend math — exactly the shape of a particle burst or a full-board flash on a big combo clear. Mitigation: use the 'Debug GPU Overdraw' developer option to visually audit (blue=1x, red/dark-red=4x+ overdraw; target: avoid red) directly on a representative low-end test device. For animation, prefer `Canvas`-based rendering for particle bursts over spawning many individual animated composables — Canvas draw calls can skip recomposition and go straight to the draw phase, and community/library benchmarks report Canvas-based particle systems handling 1000+ particles where composable-per-particle approaches degrade much sooner; use `animateDpAsState`/`animateColorAsState`/`Animatable` (designed to avoid unnecessary recomposition) rather than driving particle motion through per-frame state writes to many separate composables. If CPU+GPU work exceeds the ~16ms frame budget (60Hz), the result is visible stutter/jank and input lag — precisely the failure mode that would make a combo-cascade (many simultaneous clears, particles, and haptics firing at once) feel worse, not better, on budget hardware.
    SRC: https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/performance ; https://developer.android.com/codelabs/jetpack-compose-performance

-- low-confidence (titles only) --
  · A 'row/column about to clear' pre-highlight (showing which lines will clear before the player commits the drop, so they can plan 1-2 pieces ahead) is commonly cited as a feature in modern block-blast-style games (Block Blast, Woodoku, 1010!), functioning as the second half of the anticipation pair alongside the ghost/drop preview.

== IMPLICATIONS FOR THIS GAME ==
  → Prioritize the highest-leverage, lowest-cost juice first: (1) a squash-then-rebound scale animation on piece placement (~150-250ms total: quick squash ease-in, then rebound ease-out-back), (2) a placement 'tick' + clear 'whoosh' + escalating-pitch combo chime using the 1.06^n semitone formula, and (3) a small trauma-based screenshake (magnitude ∝ trauma², decaying linearly, capped low — a few px of translation on an 8x8 board, not 3D-game-scale shake). These three alone cover most of what 'The Art of Screenshake' / 'Juice it or Lose it' demonstrate and are cheap to build and cheap to run on any device.
  → Ghost/drop-preview (where the piece will land) is genre table-stakes — if not already implemented, treat it as near-mandatory, not a nice-to-have. A 'lines about to clear' pre-highlight is a good second-tier addition, but its sourcing as an industry-standard feature is weak (SEO-content only), so don't over-invest polish time in it before validating players actually notice/want it — a simple color-tint on the affected row/column is enough.
  → Do not over-build the haptics system for a ~70-MAU app: implement one clean tier (placement = light tick, single clear = medium thud, multi-line/combo = an escalating primitive sequence per the Android-documented bounce/wobble patterns), verify support with areAllPrimitivesSupported/areEffectsSupported, and ship a settings toggle to disable haptics/shake entirely. Composition primitives need API 30+ and support is inconsistent even then — this matters more for an India-heavy, budget-device-heavy audience than for a flagship-only launch, so a plain predefined-effect (or even no-haptics) fallback path is required, not optional.
  → Given the India-heavy, likely budget-device audience, treat GPU overdraw as a first-class risk for exactly the effects that make big combos feel good (particle bursts, full-board flashes on multi-line clears): build particle bursts with Canvas rather than a composable-per-particle, hard-cap particle count per clear event (a few dozen, not hundreds) with an object pool, and check the 'Debug GPU Overdraw' overlay on a real low/mid-tier device before shipping any full-screen flash or shake effect. A combo cascade is exactly the moment multiple juice systems (particles + shake + haptics + multiple audio voices) fire simultaneously — also exactly the moment frame drops would be most visible and most damaging to 'feel'.
  → Keep the clear animation non-blocking: per Tetris Guideline numbers, a clear/consequence animation in the ~250-600ms range (slightly longer for bigger/combo clears) is a reasonable, genre-consistent target, but don't gate acceptance of the next drag/drop on that animation finishing. Responsiveness (the player can keep placing pieces immediately) matters more to 'feel' in this genre than animation completeness — blocking input to let an animation finish is a classic way juice actively hurts a game rather than helping it.
  → Given this is a solo developer with essentially no analytics yet, don't invest in perfecting exact numeric tuning (exact ms, exact easing curve) before you can measure anything: ship the sourced starting values above as reasonable defaults, then instrument even minimal signals (session length, day-1/day-7 retention) before micro-tuning juice further. Several specifics above (score-popup duration, squash ratios, the 'lines about to clear' highlight) are genre convention/community best-practice rather than rigorously proven optimal — treat them as good defaults, not gospel, and don't burn solo-dev time chasing precision that 70 MAU can't meaningfully validate yet.

== IMPLICATIONS FOR THIS GAME ==
  → Highest value-per-hour, do first (each well under a day of solo-dev work): (1) swap the piece color set for a verified colorblind-safe palette (Okabe-Ito or IBM, hex codes above) — a pure config change; (2) add a shape/pattern/icon marker per piece color so identity never depends on color alone (WCAG 1.4.1) — this also just reads as 'nice polish' to players with normal color vision, not only a niche fix; (3) render the dragged piece offset above the fingertip instead of centered under it — a few lines changing the drag-ghost's Y translation; its absence is one of the most visible 'this feels like a cheap clone' signals in genre reviews.
  → Also very cheap and near-mandatory by Google's own Core App Quality checklist: dark theme support, ≥48dp touch targets, ≥4.5:1 (small text) / 3:1 (large text/graphics) contrast, and sizing all text in sp with a quick pass at 1.3x–2x system font scale. These are graded by name (T-Theme_Support, T-Visual_Contrast, T-Touch_Target_Size) in Google's guidelines, so fixing them is simultaneously a Play-quality-signal win and a real player-facing improvement — worth doing even at ~70 MAU because it's cheap, not because of scale.
  → Independent sound/haptics toggles plus honoring the system reduced-motion setting are both low effort in Compose: haptics/sound as two separate boolean prefs behind a settings sheet; reduced-motion is largely free if you use Compose's built-in animation APIs (Compose 1.2.0+ auto-respects it) — the only real work is auditing any hand-rolled Canvas/game-loop animation (e.g. a custom combo-explosion effect) that bypasses Compose's animation system and wouldn't otherwise pick up the system setting.
  → Predictive back and edge-to-edge are 'do this before your next targetSdk bump' items, not urgent today: predictive back just needs your pause/quit-confirmation back-press logic migrated to OnBackPressedCallback/BackHandler; edge-to-edge becomes mandatory the moment you raise targetSdk to 35, at which point any bottom action bar or FAB needs real inset padding, not a manifest opt-out (which disappears at targetSdk 36 anyway). Budget an afternoon for each when you do the SDK bump, not necessarily now if you're not bumping it yet.
  → 16 KB page size is very likely a non-issue for you and needs zero action beyond a five-minute check: it only bites apps with native (NDK) code or native-backed third-party SDKs; a pure Kotlin/Compose game has nothing to fix. Just glance at Play Console's App Bundle Explorer once before your next release to confirm there's no native-library flag.
  → Landscape/tablet layout is the one item where the 'obvious' effort is NOT worth it at your scale, and this is backed by policy, not just judgment: Android 16/17's forced-resizability rules for large screens explicitly exempt apps tagged android:appCategory="game", so a portrait-locked 8x8 grid game is not penalized by Google for staying portrait-only. Unless you have concrete evidence of tablet players bouncing (you have no analytics yet, so you don't), this is low value-per-hour work to defer — a single afternoon making sure the board doesn't look broken if someone does open it on a tablet (no crash, no absurd stretching) is enough; a real tablet-optimized layout is not.
  → Tap-to-place as an alternate input mode is a genuinely good accessibility/comfort idea (removes the sustained-drag requirement) but costs meaningfully more than everything above — a full selection/preview/cancel state machine, not a config tweak. Given ~70 MAU and no analytics telling you anyone is struggling with drag, this belongs on a 'nice later' list, not the immediate polish pass; the drag-offset fix above captures most of the same 'my thumb doesn't hide the board' benefit for a fraction of the effort.
  → The genre-analysis takeaway (Deconstructor of Fun) is less an accessibility item and more a prioritization filter for everything else: the games that win in this genre win on feedback clarity and a tight single core loop, not on feature breadth — so treat this whole list as a punch-list of small, high-clarity fixes (palette, offset, contrast, toggles) rather than a cue to add new mechanics or modes; that matches both the genre's own success pattern and what a solo dev can actually sustain.

== IMPLICATIONS FOR THIS GAME ==
  → The single highest-leverage, purely-algorithmic FTUE investment is a solvability-checked piece generator (the 'Block Peak' pattern): before dealing a 3-piece hand, run an exhaustive check that at least one ordering/placement clears something, and bias early hands (and any near-full board) toward a 'rescue' hand that guarantees a clear. This is cheap to build (no analytics, no live-ops needed), deterministic, testable offline, and is the actual mechanism behind 'guarantee an early clear' — better than hand-scripting a fixed first board, because it keeps working turn 50 and turn 500, not just turn 1.
  → Ghost/preview should show more than a drop silhouette — highlight which full rows/columns a placement would complete before the player commits. That single piece of UI is doing most of the 'teaching' work in this genre; with near-zero tutorial text, this preview plus a piece generator that makes early clears likely IS the tutorial.
  → Skip building a scripted tutorial overlay. Genre convention (Puzzledorf postmortem, GameRefinery) says: make the first 1–2 pieces impossible to place wrong (huge open board, single-cell-friendly shapes), remove any text, and reserve a one-line contextual hint (e.g. 'Fill a row or column to clear it') for the moment a player stalls (no successful placement for N seconds, or repeated invalid-drop attempts) rather than showing it upfront to everyone — the insight that 'players who get stuck are the ones who'll actually read a hint' argues for pull-based hints over a forced first-run tour.
  → Deliberately engineer the first combo/multi-line clear as a designed beat: bias the first ~5–10 dealt hands (via the same solvability-checked generator) toward setups where a double-line or L-shape clear is very likely within the first minute, and make sure the audio/visual payoff (screen feedback, score pop, brief pause) is the biggest 'moment' in the whole first session — this is the genre's actual hook, more than any explanatory text would be.
  → Do not gate coins/shop/streak behind or inside the first session. Let the player play several pieces uninterrupted; if you have a starter currency or booster, hand one to them for free during play (so they learn what it does) rather than pitching it; introduce a daily-streak/return hook only at the natural end of the first session (app backgrounding or a clear stopping point), not as a popup before they've placed a piece.
  → Treat ads as a distinct, block-puzzle-specific day-0 risk, not generic FTUE noise: real App Store complaints for this exact genre show that (a) ad frequency and (b) ad-refresh causing mid-drag input lag are named uninstall reasons. At minimum, do not run interstitials in the first session, and be careful that any ad refresh/load never fires during an active drag gesture — that failure mode (misplacing a piece because of ad-triggered lag) is a uniquely avoidable, uniquely bad first impression for a drag-to-place game.
  → Be honest that most of the numeric FTUE benchmarks in circulation (60s-to-core-loop, 90s-to-aha, D1 20-33%, tutorial completion ~80%, 'up to 50% retention lift from onboarding') come from marketing/growth blogs aggregating unnamed or loosely-cited studies, and the puzzle-genre session-length figure alone varies roughly 5x between sources found in this research. At ~70 monthly active devices, none of these numbers are usable as targets to hit or A/B against anyway — there isn't the sample size for a real D1/D2 funnel. The actionable version of 'benchmarks' at this scale is qualitative: sit next to (or screen-record) a handful of actual users on their very first launch and watch whether they place a piece within seconds, whether they get an early clear unprompted, and whether they ever look confused — that single-digit-N observation will teach more than importing an industry percentage.
  → Since there's no analytics yet, instrument the two or three numbers that matter for exactly this FTUE work — first-session length, whether the player reached a first line-clear, whether/when they hit a game-over — before investing further in tuning any of the above; without even minimal telemetry it's impossible to tell 'no one churns because the game is fine' apart from 'no one churns because there are only 70 users total.'

    DETAIL: This is the strongest evidence in the whole research set for a specific mechanic change causally tied to a specific retention-metric move, and it directly answers 'streak systems: loss aversion vs forgiveness' — the key design lesson is DECOUPLE the trivial daily action that keeps the streak alive from the harder 'real' daily goal; that decoupling alone was worth +40% streak-holder population.
    SRC: https://www.digia.tech/post/duolingo-habit-forming-reminders-retention-architecture/

[10] (medium) Duolingo's 'Streak Freeze' (forgiveness item) doesn't change short-term day-to-day survival much, but compounds: users with freeze access maintain streaks 4.5x longer by day 21, and for users past the 14-day mark the gap widens to 30.63 days average streak length (with freezes) vs 18.87 days (without); introducing Streak Freeze reduced churn by 21% among users at risk of breaking their streak.
    DETAIL: Direct evidence for the 'forgiveness' side of the streak design question: forgiveness mechanics don't matter for casual/short streaks, but become the dominant retention lever for players who've already built a habit — i.e. give freezes primarily to protect established streaks (14d+), not to prop up day-2 users.
    SRC: https://trophy.so/blog/streaks-feature-gamification-examples

[11] (medium) In a peer-reviewed academic study of 51,104 players in an online multiplayer game (Park, Cha, Kwak & Chen, WWW'17), achievement-type features were the strongest predictor of retention for new-to-mid-level players, but social/friend features became the single most predictive factor of longevity once players reached the game's highest level — i.e. the predictor of 'staying' changes across the player lifecycle.
    DETAIL: Caveat: this is an MMO/online multiplayer study, not a casual single-player puzzle game, so the 'social features matter most at max level' half transfers weakly to a solo block-puzzle game with no social graph. The 'achievements drive early/mid retention' half is the more directly transferable finding — it supports investing in achievements early rather than waiting for a critical-mass community.
    SRC: https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08005

[12] (medium) Progressive daily-reward calendars/thresholds (login calendar with escalating gifts, growing daily bonus, quest-completion bonus, or a single daily quest with reward 'thresholds') are present in nearly 50% of iOS US Top-100-grossing games vs under 33% of games outside the Top 100 — an adoption/correlation signal, not a controlled retention-lift number.
    DETAIL: Useful as a prioritization signal (this feature correlates with top-grossing status) but the source itself states no quantified retention or revenue uplift number and no streak-reset/forgiveness policy detail — don't oversell this as causal.
    SRC: https://www.gamerefinery.com/feature-spotlight-progression-daily-rewards/

[13] (high) GameRefinery's genre analysis of lapsed-player re-engagement identifies 4 recurring mechanic families across mobile games: (1) 'welcome back' one-time gift bundles (boosts/lives/currency) — and for CASUAL games specifically, 'a well-designed welcome-back gift is often the only thing needed to re-engage players'; (2) monetizing the return via time-limited comeback offers; (3) short (e.g. 5-day) returning-player mission/buff chains with disproportionately generous rewards; (4) friend-system reactivation incentives (e.g. PUBG Mobile's Recall event rewarding players for re-activating friends).
    DETAIL: For a casual single-player puzzle game, mechanic family #1 is explicitly called out as usually sufficient on its own — i.e. a solo dev doesn't need the full 4-mechanic apparatus (which assumes social graphs and monetization funnels a small game doesn't have).
    SRC: https://www.gamerefinery.com/four-ways-how-mobile-games-re-engage-lapsed-players/

[14] (medium) Real-time competitive/ranked systems need a meaningful concurrent population to function: backend-as-a-service leaderboard vendors design 'bucketed' event leaderboards specifically because a raw global leaderboard needs a large population to feel fair, instead grouping each player into a cohort of roughly 25-50 similarly-matched opponents per event cycle; large mobile games' ranked tournament groups (e.g. Clash of Clans-style ranked battle groups) are sized around ~100 players per group.
    DETAIL: This is the concrete population math behind 'which layers are pointless below ~1,000 DAU': even ONE healthy tournament/ranked cohort wants on the order of 25-100 concurrent, comparably-skilled players refreshing regularly. A 70-MAU app cannot fill even a single such cohort once, let alone every event cycle — so live tournaments, ranked ladders, and friend-vs-friend competitive leaderboards are structurally non-functional (not just 'less effective') below roughly four-figure DAU, not just MAU.
    SRC: https://heroiclabs.com/docs/hiro/concepts/event-leaderboards/

-- low-confidence (titles only) --
  · Several SEO/aggregator blogs (Segwise, Appagent, Playio, GameGrowthAdvisor) cite much higher puzzle-genre numbers — D1 ~31.85%, D7 ~12.18%, D30 ~5.35% — sourced from unnamed/unclear datasets, materially disagreeing with GameAnalytics' own published figures for the same genre/year.
  · Catch-up/comeback design principle: returning players churn again quickly if they feel overwhelmed by how much changed while they were away, so effective comeback rewards scale with absence length (Ni no Kuni: Cross Worlds gives a bigger 'you missed events' gift the longer the player was gone) rather than a single flat welcome-back reward.
  · Industry commentary broadly claims friends-based leaderboards outperform global leaderboards for retention/behavior (global boards demoralize players ranked far from the top), but a peer-reviewed academic study on the citizen-science game Quantum Moves comparing no-leaderboard / top-5-leaderboard / relative-leaderboard conditions found NO strong preference in retention or performance across conditions.
  · Standard mobile-game re-engagement/win-back cadence guidance: send 3-5 re-engagement pushes over a 30-60 day window, spaced at least 5-7 days apart, with the first push timed 3-14 days after the player goes inactive; contacting a lapsed player across multiple channels (push + email + SMS + in-app) within the same 24 hours causes unsubscribes rather than returns; the highest-performing re-engagement message is about NEW CONTENT ('the world you loved has grown'), not discount offers.

== IMPLICATIONS FOR THIS GAME ==
  → DO build (cheap, single-player, no backend/population needed, and each has direct evidence above): a daily seeded challenge board with its own streak counter; 1-2 forgiveness tokens ('streak freeze') that protect an ALREADY-established streak (7d+) rather than propping up day-2 players, since Duolingo's own data shows freezes barely matter early but roughly 1.6x a streak's length once it's past ~14 days; and make sure the action that keeps the streak alive is trivially easy (clear one line, not 'get a great score') — Duolingo's single biggest reported streak-population win (+40%+) came exactly from decoupling the 'keep the streak' bar from the 'real' daily-goal bar.
  → DO build: a small set of fast, first-session-reachable achievements plus a modest number of longer-horizon ones (theme unlocks, piece-collection sets, a personal best/progress-tracking screen) — every one of these is single-player, has zero population dependency, and matches both the sort/block-puzzle-specific design checklist and the academic finding that achievement-type features are what drives EARLY/MID retention (the social-feature-matters-more-at-max-level half of that same study doesn't apply — there's no social graph at 70 MAU).
  → DO build, but scope down hard: a finite 'adventure'/level-set layered on top of the existing endless mode is the exact same architecture Block Blast (same genre) used, but a solo dev should ship dozens of curated levels, not the 5,000+ a large studio's live-ops team maintains — the goal is giving players a structured on-ramp and a sense of a 'destination,' not matching a content-treadmill you can't sustain.
  → DO build: one 'welcome back' gift (extra hints/undos/a streak-freeze/a cosmetic) triggered by a simple local last-active-timestamp check. GameRefinery's own genre analysis says this single mechanic is 'often the only thing needed' to re-engage lapsed players in a casual game — you do not need the fuller monetization/mission-chain/friend-reactivation apparatus that assumes a live-ops team and a paying audience.
  → DON'T build real-time competitive layers: global leaderboards-as-live-competition, ranked ladders, tournaments, guild/clan or friend-vs-friend features are the mechanics most clearly gated by population math in this research — vendor-designed 'fair' leaderboard cohorts run ~25-50 concurrent similarly-matched players, and major games size ranked tournament groups around ~100; 70 total MONTHLY active users cannot fill even one such cohort once, let alone refresh it every cycle, so these features would ship dead/empty, which reads as worse than not having them at all. A purely cosmetic, non-competitive ALL-TIME high-score list (no live ranking, no opponent, just 'your best ever') is the only leaderboard-shaped thing worth keeping at this scale.
  → DON'T build a LiveOps event calendar in the Candy Crush/Royal Match sense (weekly tournaments, seasonal albums, production-team-run limited-time events) — that apparatus is built and run by dedicated live-ops teams processing continuous population data; for a solo dev the honest analog is a much slower, manual content refresh (e.g. a new board theme or challenge pack roughly monthly) rather than chasing weekly cadence.
  → DON'T build a multi-channel win-back push/CRM pipeline (segmented by spend/progression, 3-5 timed pushes, email+SMS+in-app) — that's infrastructure sized for populations where a 1-2% lift is worth engineering effort; at 70 MAU the entire expected win-back yield from such a system is a handful of sessions, not worth the build/maintenance cost. A single well-timed local notification ('your streak is waiting' / 'new theme unlocked') captures most of the same value GameRefinery attributes to the highest-performing message type (new-content framing beats discount-offer framing) at near-zero engineering cost.
  → Biggest structural gap found in the research relative to this project: 'no analytics yet' is itself the highest-priority fix before adding more progression layers. Every single finding above that ties a design change to a metric move (Royal Match's Propeller Rush event, Duolingo's Streak Wager, the streak-decoupling change) was only knowable BECAUSE the team had D1/D7/D14/D30 cohort tracking and A/B infra. Without at least a free-tier analytics/cohort-retention hookup (e.g. Firebase Analytics — free at this scale), a solo dev adding streaks/achievements/adventure-mode/comeback-gifts will genuinely not be able to tell which of these layers is doing anything, which defeats the point of prioritizing by 'what moved D7/D30' at all.
  → General diagnostic lens worth adopting once instrumentation exists (repeated across multiple secondary sources, treat as a heuristic not a law): a weak D1 points to onboarding/first-session problems, a weak D7 points to a missing daily-habit hook (streaks/daily challenge — the highest-leverage, lowest-cost layer identified here), and a weak D30 points to a missing depth/meta layer (adventure mode, collections, content refresh) — so if forced to sequence the backlog, streak+daily-challenge should ship before adventure-mode/collections, which should ship before any comeback/win-back tooling.

== IMPLICATIONS FOR THIS GAME ==
  → Copy the table-stakes core loop exactly (it's genuinely good design, not just habit): untimed play, 3 pieces dealt per turn, clear on full row/column (add 3x3-region clears only if you want Woodoku/Blockudoku's extra depth), and a single 'unstick me' tool (a hammer/shovel that destroys one block or small area). This is the small set of features that accounts for most of why the genre feels good, and it's cheap to build well.
  → Implement the board as a 64-bit bitmask (one Long for an 8x8 grid), not a 2D array — this is exactly what a real solver author (blokie, C++) chose for performance, and in Kotlin it makes placement-legality and full-line-clear checks a few bitwise ops instead of nested loops; it also makes 'find a hint/valid placement' (useful for your own hint feature) trivial to brute-force.
  → Do NOT build a Block-Blast-style Adventure mode with thousands of hand/procedurally-generated levels, and do NOT build Blockudoku's four-system LiveOps stack (Endless + Daily + Seasonal Events + Tournaments). Both are built by teams running continuous A/B tests against tens of millions of DAU; at ~70 MAU the labor cost per player is astronomical and a near-empty tournament/leaderboard actively feels worse than no leaderboard at all.
  → DO build Woodoku's cheapest high-leverage retention feature: a single hand-authored Daily Challenge plus a visible monthly calendar of 'days completed' — it's one puzzle a day (trivial content cost), gives a genuine reason to open the app daily, and the calendar itself (not a server-side streak counter) is the whole retention hook.
  → Skip real monetization infrastructure (interstitials, rewarded video, IAP store) for now. Block Blast's ad revenue (~$584k/day) only exists because of ~100M+ MAU; at 70 MAU there is no meaningful ad revenue to be had, and every review-mined complaint across all three games centers on ad frequency/length — so the same 'watch an ad to revive' pattern you might copy for UX reasons (comeback mechanic) should almost certainly not actually be wired to a real ad SDK yet. Use a free, no-cost 'revive' (e.g., once per session, clear a few of the worst-placed cells) purely as a game-feel feature.
  → For 'help when stuck,' Blockudoku's design (hint costs -10% of your score, undo has a small fixed number of uses) is a smarter default than an unlimited free hammer: it keeps the tool available without letting it trivialize the puzzle, and it needs zero currency/economy system to implement — just a session-scoped counter and a score multiplier check.
  → For social features, don't build your own leaderboard backend. Use Google Play Games Services (free, built into Android) for leaderboards/achievements — it gives you the same 'compete with friends' feeling Blockudoku gets from Game Center, with effectively zero infrastructure cost, and it still works fine (arguably better, since it's opt-in and unobtrusive) at very small player counts.
  → Invest disproportionately in game-feel polish (haptics on every clear, sound layering, a satisfying clear animation) — this is cheap for a solo dev to get right and is explicitly called out (Shield article) as central to why Block Blast 'feels' compulsive despite having almost no other systems. It's likely the highest ROI-per-hour item on this whole list.
  → Cosmetic themes (board/tile color palettes) are worth doing cheaply and are common to all three games, but keep them purely visual with no gameplay impact (as all three confirm) and gate them behind simple play-time/achievement milestones rather than building a currency/store system around them.
  → Treat Woodoku's and Blockudoku's 'ads block you from even seeing your final board' and 'unskippable ad after every few moves' complaints as an explicit anti-pattern list — if/when you do add monetization later, never gate the player's ability to see their own end-of-game board behind an ad, and never show forced ads more often than roughly once per genuine session-completion event.
  → The genre's real differentiation frontier right now (per Deconstructor of Fun and Naavik) is 'themes and deeper toolkits' and authored-level puzzle design (Color Block Jam), not more LiveOps systems — for a solo dev, a well-designed handful of daily/weekly hand-crafted challenge puzzles with a unique twist (e.g., a pre-filled board, a special color-bomb piece) is a more realistic differentiator than trying to out-system three companies with dedicated LiveOps teams.
  → Session-length data here is not reliable enough to design against directly (only Block Blast's ~12 min average is a solid, sourced number; genre-wide 'session length' stats found are ambiguous about daily-total vs. single-sitting). Design for 'easy in, easy out' as a principle (get to a meaningful decision within seconds, let the player leave without penalty at any point) rather than trying to hit a specific minute target.
